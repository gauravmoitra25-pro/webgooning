Kolkata

A river city of trams, bridges, and everyday poetry — the place I still call home.

Scroll to explore

Kolkata doesn't perform for visitors. It simply continues, the way it always has.

For over three centuries, Kolkata has been the intellectual and cultural heart of eastern India — first as the capital of British India, later as the city that gave the country Nobel laureates, filmmakers, and a literary tradition few places can match.

It moves at its own pace: mornings over tea and newspapers, afternoons in College Street's bookstalls, evenings along the Hooghly as the sky turns the colour of the water. This page is a small, personal tour of three things that, to me, are simply what Kolkata looks like.

Howrah Bridge

Every morning, close to a hundred thousand vehicles and more than a hundred thousand people cross this cantilever bridge over the Hooghly River — one of the busiest of its kind anywhere in the world. It was assembled without a single nut or bolt; its steel is held together entirely by rivets, a detail that still surprises people the first time they hear it.

At sunrise, with river traffic below and the city waking up around it, it's hard to imagine Kolkata without its silhouette.

Victoria Memorial

Built from the same white Makrana marble as the Taj Mahal, the Victoria Memorial took fifteen years to complete and now serves as a museum rather than a monument to empire. Its domed silhouette rises over sixty-four acres of gardens, reflecting pools, and quiet paths.

On any given evening those grounds fill with families, students, and couples simply out for a walk — a building that has outlived the purpose it was built for, and become something the city actually uses.

Rosogolla

No Bengali celebration is complete without a plate of these — spongy balls of fresh cottage cheese, barely sweetened, floating in clear syrup. The sweet is generally credited to Nabin Chandra Das, a Kolkata confectioner working in the 1860s.

West Bengal and neighbouring Odisha have spent years — and a formal GI dispute — arguing over whose rosogolla came first. Either way, the version made in Kolkata's old sweet shops is the one I grew up on.
